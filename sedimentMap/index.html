How these results were produced

About this dashboard

SCIMAP maps where diffuse pollution — fine sediment and the nutrients and pathogens that travel with it — is most likely to come from, and where in the river it is most likely to be felt. It asks two questions of every part of the catchment: can the land give up material, and will that material reach the river? The answers come from the terrain, the land cover and the rainfall, so the method works anywhere, with or without water quality monitoring.

The approach

Four steps, each a layer you can switch on. The bar under a step shows its colours on the map.

  1. 1

    Erosion risk

    Can the land give up material?

    How hard running water can work on the ground — the slope times the area draining through each cell — weighted by how vulnerable its land cover is.

  2. ×
  3. 2

    Connectivity

    Will it reach the river?

    A cell is only as connected as the driest point on its route down to the channel. Wetness comes from the terrain and the rainfall.

  4. =
  5. 3

    Source risk

    What can each cell deliver?

    Erosion risk × connectivity: high only where the land is both erodible and connected. The critical source areas are its top 5%.

  6. →
  7. 4

    In-channel risk

    How concentrated is it in the river?

    Source risk added up downstream, divided by the rainfall-weighted area upstream that dilutes it. High where risky, connected land makes up much of what the water has drained.

Every score is relative: it ranks places within this catchment, so the maps show where risk is concentrated rather than how much pollution there is. Steps 1 and 2 are why two places with the same land use can matter very differently: a ploughed field on a steep slope that drains straight to a ditch is a problem, and the same field on a flat hilltop, whose runoff must cross a long, well-drained slope to reach a stream, mostly is not.

The flood layers follow the same logic for water rather than sediment: where runoff is generated and how quickly it reaches the river, across a set of rainfall patterns.

The nature-based solutions layers build on these results: a decision key reads each cell’s risk and landscape position and recommends measures that fit it.

Working through the results

One way from the river back to the land, and out to the field.

  1. Find the reaches at risk. The map opens on the stream network, coloured by in-channel risk. Click the brightest, thickest reaches, or open Analysis and look at Priority reaches.
  2. Trace the risk to the land. Switch on Erosion risk × connectivity and follow the high values upstream of those reaches: that is the land delivering the risk.
  3. Shortlist places to act. Analysis, Where to act first, lists the critical source areas: the patches of land holding most of the source risk. Select one to go to it on the map.
  4. Choose the kind of measure. An area’s colour — or Erosion risk and Network connectivity side by side — says whether to work on the source (ground cover, soil management, land use) or the pathway (buffer strips, hedges, ponds, breaking up flow routes). The nature-based solutions layers suggest a specific measure for each place.
  5. Check on the ground. Plan a visit with the 3D view and the satellite basemap. Local knowledge and field survey confirm, or correct, what the model suggests.
  6. Share it. Print a report or save it as Markdown, or pass on the whole dashboard folder: it opens in any browser, offline.

Finding your way around

Map
Tick layers in the panel on the left: the one higher in the list is drawn on top, and the slider under each fades it. Move the pointer over the map to read every layer’s value at that spot (bottom left); click to pin the reading. Click a reach for its rank and length. The layers button at the top right changes the background: shaded relief works offline, the Esri maps need an internet connection. Save image keeps the map as you see it.
3D terrain
The same layers draped over the land. Drag to turn it, right-drag to move it, scroll to zoom. Raise the vertical exaggeration to bring out gentle relief, and move the sun to pick out slopes; the animated flow traces water down the network. Save an image, or a 3D model to view in AR on a phone or tablet or to 3D print.
Analysis
The catchment in figures. Where to act first lists the critical source areas. Risk distribution and Risk concentration show how evenly a layer is spread: the more the curve bows, the more of the risk sits on a small share of the land, and the more targeting pays. Reading the results with care

  • Relative, not absolute. The scores rank places within this catchment. They say where risk is concentrated, not how much pollution there is, and are not comparable between dashboards made from different runs.
  • Colours are stretched. Each layer’s colours run between its 5th and 95th percentiles, so the top and bottom 5% share the end colours. Move the pointer over the map to read the actual values.
  • As good as the inputs. The elevation model, land cover, rainfall and the risk weight given to each land cover all shape the result; they are listed under Provenance. Field drains, culverts and tracks that the elevation model cannot see are missing from the flow routes.
  • One value per cell. Anything smaller than a cell, such as a narrow buffer strip, is averaged in with its surroundings.
  • A guide to where to look. Use the maps to prioritise, alongside local knowledge and field survey, not in place of them.
